# Skill: Difficult Conversations

**Domain**: Conflict Resolution
**Owner**: `subagents/engineering-manager.md`
**Cadence**: On-demand — invoked when a high-stakes conversation is required and the EM needs preparation before attempting it

---

## Purpose

Prepare and execute high-stakes conversations — those involving feedback, conflict, performance, role change, or interpersonal friction — so that the conversation produces genuine understanding rather than surface compliance, defensive shutdown, or unresolved tension.

**What the EM does automatically**: Before a difficult conversation, the EM:
1. Identifies the specific outcome the conversation must produce (not "it went well" — the actual behavior change or shared understanding)
2. Prepares the opening frame so the other person understands the purpose within the first 60 seconds
3. Anticipates defensive responses and designs a path through them
4. Distinguishes facts from interpretations in their own preparation — this prevents moralizing
5. Produces: Root cause, conversation strategy, risks, desired outcome, and follow-up actions

**Ethical principle**: A difficult conversation is not a performance of authority. It is an attempt to understand and be understood. The EM who enters a conversation knowing they are right has already failed. The purpose of the conversation is to reach a shared picture of reality — which may require the EM to update their own view. Feedback that cannot be heard is not feedback; it is noise. The EM's job is to make it hearable.

## Analysis

### Step 1 — Classify the Conversation Type

Different conversation types require different opening frames and pacing:

```
Type A — Feedback conversation
  Purpose: Specific behavior must change
  Risk: Person becomes defensive; the behavior that prompted the conversation is not actually named
  Frame: "I want to give you some specific feedback about {{behavior}}.
  My goal is for this to be useful to you."
  → Lead with impact, not judgment

Type B — Performance conversation
  Purpose: Pattern of underperformance must be addressed and a shared plan created
  Risk: EM is vague; person leaves unclear what "improving" looks like
  Frame: "I want to talk about a pattern I've been observing in {{area}}.
  I want to understand your perspective and agree on what needs to change."
  → Be specific about the pattern; invite their read; agree on the bar

Type C — Conflict conversation
  Purpose: Tension between two parties must be named, understood, and addressed
  Risk: EM diagnoses without hearing; person feels accused; the EM's view becomes the verdict
  Frame: "There's some tension between you and {{person}} that I want to address directly.
  I want to start by understanding your experience."
  → Start with their account, not yours

Type D — Structural change conversation
  Purpose: Role, scope, team, or reporting change must be communicated and processed
  Risk: Person hears the change but not the rationale; feels dismissed; doesn't process it in the conversation
  Frame: "I have a change to tell you about that affects your role. I want to explain
  the decision and give you space to respond to it."
  → Deliver the change clearly first; make space for their reaction second

Type E — Repair conversation
  Purpose: EM made an error, broke trust, or owes acknowledgment; relationship must be repaired
  Risk: Apology is partial; EM qualifies it ("I'm sorry you felt..."); the repair doesn't land
  Frame: "I want to come back to what happened with {{incident}} because I don't think
  I handled it well. I'd like to acknowledge what I got wrong."
  → Take full responsibility for the EM's part; do not split it
```

### Step 2 — Separate Facts from Interpretations

```
Before the conversation, the EM must prepare two columns:

FACTS (what can be observed, documented, or agreed upon):
  "You missed the sprint planning meeting on Tuesday without notice."
  "The PR review was completed 4 days after the deadline you committed to."
  "Three team members have mentioned this pattern in 1:1s over the last month."

INTERPRETATIONS (what the EM has concluded from facts — not to be stated as facts):
  "You don't take commitments seriously."
  "You're disengaged."
  "You don't respect your teammates."

Rule: Lead with facts. Interpretations belong only in questions:
  "When I see {{fact}}, I wonder {{interpretation}}.
  What's your read on what's happening?"

Why this matters: An interpretation stated as a fact triggers defensiveness.
A question about an interpretation invites the other person into the analysis.
The person who was there knows something the EM doesn't.
```

### Step 3 — Design the Conversation Structure

```
Phase 1 — Opening (60–90 seconds)
  State the purpose: What this conversation is about and why it matters
  State the intent: That the EM wants to understand, not just deliver
  State the format: "I want to share what I've observed, then hear your perspective"
  
  Signal: Purpose is clear; tone is direct but not punitive; the other person knows
  where this is going but doesn't feel ambushed

Phase 2 — Share the Observation
  State the specific fact(s)
  State the impact: On the team, the work, or the relationship
  Do NOT state the verdict: The EM's interpretation is a hypothesis, not a conclusion

Phase 3 — Invite Their Perspective
  "What's your read on this?"
  "What was happening from your side?"
  "Is there context I don't have?"
  
  Critical: The EM must listen for real. If something they hear changes the picture,
  acknowledge it and update. Pretending to listen while waiting to respond is not listening.

Phase 4 — Reach a Shared Understanding
  Summarize what was heard from both sides
  Identify where the perspectives agree and where they differ
  Do NOT force agreement: "I hear that you see it differently. Here's what I'm asking for
  regardless: {{specific behavior or commitment}}"

Phase 5 — Close with a Specific Agreement
  Name the specific change or commitment
  Name when you'll check back in
  Do not end with vague language: "I'm not asking you to be perfect — I'm asking for {{specific}}"
  
  If no agreement is reached: name that explicitly
  "We're not at an agreement yet. I want to give you time to think. Let's pick this up
  by {{date}}. What I need to know by then is {{specific}}"
```

### Step 4 — Prepare for Defensive Responses

```
Common defensive responses and how to stay in the conversation:

Response: "That's not fair."
Stay in: "Tell me what's not fair about it. I want to understand your read."
Trap to avoid: Defending the fairness of your position — you'll lose and the
               conversation will derail into a fairness debate

Response: Silence or shutdown
Stay in: "I notice you've gone quiet. That's okay — this is a lot to take in.
          What would be helpful right now?"
Trap to avoid: Filling the silence by continuing to talk; the shutdown needs space

Response: Counter-accusation ("You do X too")
Stay in: "I want to hear that — and I'd like to address it separately.
          Right now I'd like to stay with what I raised. Is that okay?"
Trap to avoid: Defending yourself — now you're in a swap of accusations and the
               original issue disappears

Response: Minimizing ("It's not a big deal")
Stay in: "Here's why it matters to me: {{specific impact}}.
          I'm not saying it was intentional — I'm saying it had an effect."
Trap to avoid: Debating whether it's a "big deal" instead of addressing the impact

Response: Agreeing too quickly ("You're right, I'll fix it")
Stay in: Slow down — premature agreement often means disengagement, not commitment
         "I want to make sure we're on the same page. Can you tell me specifically
         what you're going to do differently?"
Trap to avoid: Accepting surface agreement and leaving without a real plan
```

## Decision Tree

```
What does the conversation preparation reveal?

├── INSUFFICIENT FACTS
│   └── Do not have the conversation yet
│       Gather specific, observable instances before proceeding
│       Vague feedback is not feedback — it's unfair to the recipient
│       Frame to yourself: "What exactly did I observe? When? What was the impact?"

├── EM IS THE AGGRIEVED PARTY — TYPE E (REPAIR)
│   └── This conversation is harder because you're vulnerable
│       Write out what you got wrong before the conversation
│       Do NOT combine apology with "but here's what you did wrong"
│       → Repair first. Separate conversation if needed for the other issue.

├── TYPE B (PERFORMANCE) — PATTERN, NOT INCIDENT
│   └── A single incident does not warrant a performance conversation
│       Three or more related instances, or one severe instance, warrants it
│       Be prepared to name: what "good" looks like specifically
│       → If underperformance has already been discussed: escalate the frame:
│          "We've talked about this. I need to be direct: this is now at the level
│          where it affects your standing on the team."

├── SIGNIFICANT POWER DIFFERENTIAL (EM speaking to senior IC or skip-level)
│   └── Power differential changes how feedback lands
│       More important to open space, not more important to deliver the message
│       Lead with questions: "How are you experiencing this project?"
│       Let them arrive at the issue before naming it — if they can

├── THE CONVERSATION IS ABOUT CONDUCT (TYPE 4 OR LEVEL 4)
│   └── Do not conduct this alone
│       Loop in HR before the conversation, not after
│       → Invoke: conflict-diagnosis.md for escalation routing

└── PREPARED — PROCEED
    └── Confirm: facts separated from interpretations
        Opening frame written out
        Desired outcome named specifically
        Follow-up mechanism in place
        → Execute the five-phase conversation structure
```

## Failure Modes

| Mode | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Interpretation as verdict** | EM states conclusions ("You don't care about quality") instead of observations ("Three PRs were merged without tests this sprint") | Rewrite using the facts column. State the fact; ask about the interpretation. |
| **Vague closing** | Conversation ends with "I hope things improve" | Name the specific behavior expected and the date for check-in. Vague conversations produce vague changes. |
| **Avoiding the conversation** | EM softens the message until it no longer carries the weight of the concern | If it needed to be said, say it. Softening to the point of inaudibility is avoidance with extra steps. |
| **Defensiveness taken as the answer** | Person reacts defensively; EM backs down and the conversation ends without reaching the issue | Defensive response is a response, not a resolution. Stay in the conversation. |
| **Repair conversation split** | EM apologizes and then immediately raises what the other person did wrong | Repair first. Combining apology with accusation cancels the apology. Two separate conversations if needed. |
