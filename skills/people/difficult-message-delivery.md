# Skill: Difficult Message Delivery

**Domain**: People Intelligence
**Owner**: `subagents/engineering-manager.md`
**Cadence**: On-demand — layoffs, org restructures, promotion denials, performance outcomes, role eliminations

---

## Purpose

Deliver messages that are hard for the recipient to hear in a way that preserves their dignity, your relationship integrity, and the team's trust in leadership — even when the message itself is bad.

The hardest messages in management are not hard because they are technically complex. They are hard because they carry stakes for the recipient's livelihood, identity, or sense of fairness. The EM's job is not to make these messages feel good — it is to ensure they land with clarity, respect, and honesty, in a way that gives the person the best possible chance to process and act.

**What the EM does automatically**: In difficult message delivery, the EM:
1. Delivers the message directly and early — does not bury the lead or delay to protect their own discomfort
2. Separates fact from opinion, and owns which is which
3. Creates space for the recipient's reaction without filling it
4. Answers the questions the person actually has — not the questions that are easier to answer
5. Defines clear next steps so the person leaves the conversation knowing what happens from here

**What this skill does NOT cover**: Routine negative feedback, performance coaching conversations, or conflict resolution. Those have their own skills. This skill covers organizational-level messages — the ones where the EM is delivering a decision, not opening a dialogue about one.

**Ethical principle**: The person receiving this message deserves to hear it from you first, directly, and with enough specificity to understand what is happening. Vague soft-pedaling is not kindness — it forces the person to ask the clarifying questions that they should not have to ask after a message this significant. Deliver it clearly.

---

## Inputs

### Required
| Input | Description |
|---|---|
| **The message** | Exactly what is happening — one specific sentence |
| **Why it is happening** | The true reason — what you are authorized to share |
| **What happens next** | The immediate timeline and the next concrete step for this person |
| **What the person can ask you** | The scope of questions you are prepared to answer |

### Optional
| Input | Description |
|---|---|
| **Legal/HR constraints** | What you cannot say and why |
| **Whether others are affected** | Is this individual or part of a broader action? |
| **Resources being offered** | Severance, transition support, references, etc. |
| **Your relationship history with this person** | How long you've worked together, what you owe them in terms of honesty |

## Analysis

### Step 1 — Classify the Message Type

```
Message type determines the preparation requirements and delivery structure.

TYPE 1 — LAYOFF / ROLE ELIMINATION
  What it is: The person's role is ending. This is typically final, non-negotiable,
  and involves legal/HR coordination.
  Who delivers it: EM + HR partner (in most organizations)
  Preparation requirement: Maximum — legal review, severance clarity, reference
  commitment, day-of logistics confirmed before the conversation
  Unique challenge: The person will hear the first sentence and stop processing
  the rest. Plan for this. The first sentence is the message.
  What they need immediately: When is their last day? What happens to their
  access? Can they tell people themselves?

TYPE 2 — PROMOTION DENIAL
  What it is: The person was considered for promotion and did not receive it.
  This cycle or this period.
  Who delivers it: EM (primary), no HR involvement unless the decision is contested
  Preparation requirement: High — specific gaps named, specific evidence, clear
  and honest timeline for what changes
  Unique challenge: The person may have expected promotion. The gap between
  expectation and outcome is often more painful than the gap itself. Address
  the expectation directly, not just the outcome.
  What they need immediately: What specifically is missing? What would change
  the outcome? When is the next opportunity?

TYPE 3 — ORG RESTRUCTURE / REPORTING CHANGE
  What it is: The team structure, reporting line, or team membership is changing.
  The person may not have been consulted.
  Who delivers it: EM (sometimes with skip-level present)
  Preparation requirement: High — rationale must be credible, timeline must be
  firm, the person's role in the new structure must be clear
  Unique challenge: The person may feel the change was done to them, not for them.
  The feeling of not being consulted on something that affects their career is
  a trust injury that is distinct from the content of the change.
  What they need immediately: Why was this decided? What does it mean for my role?
  Was I considered? Do I have any input?

TYPE 4 — PERFORMANCE OUTCOME (PIP, demotion, role change for performance)
  What it is: A structured consequence of a performance problem — a PIP, a
  role change, a demotion, or a formal warning.
  Who delivers it: EM + HR partner
  Preparation requirement: Maximum — documentation must be in place before this
  conversation; nothing new is surfaced for the first time here
  Unique challenge: This conversation should contain no surprises. If the person
  is hearing the performance concern for the first time in a PIP conversation,
  the EM has already failed. This skill covers the message delivery — prior
  coaching conversations are covered by `skills/people/feedback.md` and
  `skills/performance/performance-improvement.md`
  What they need immediately: What specifically is required? By when? What support
  is being provided? What happens if expectations are met / not met?

TYPE 5 — GENERAL ORG CHANGE (team merger, product cancellation, office change)
  What it is: An organizational decision that affects this person's work context.
  Who delivers it: EM
  Preparation requirement: Medium — rationale must be credible; questions about
  impact must be answerable
  What they need immediately: How does this affect my day-to-day? My team?
  My career trajectory?
```

### Step 2 — Prepare the Message Architecture

```
Before the conversation, build the message architecture. Not a script — an architecture.
A script produces a robotic delivery. An architecture produces a prepared, honest one.

THE FOUR PARTS OF EVERY DIFFICULT MESSAGE

PART 1 — THE LEAD (3–5 seconds)
  The most direct version of the message, delivered at the start.
  No warm-up, no softening preamble that makes the recipient wait anxiously.

  ✗ "I wanted to talk to you because we've been thinking about the team a lot..."
  ✓ "I need to tell you that your role is being eliminated."

  ✗ "The promotion committee met last week and there was a lot of discussion..."
  ✓ "I want to let you know that you're not being promoted this cycle."

  Rule: The recipient is scanning every sentence looking for the message. Give it to
        them immediately. Anything before the message increases anxiety and
        decreases the dignity of the delivery.

PART 2 — THE REASON (honest, authorized, specific)
  The real reason. Not the sanitized version.
  What you are authorized to share. Not more than that — but not less than that, either.

  For layoffs: "This is a cost reduction. [N] roles are being eliminated across the org."
    — Not: "This was a difficult business decision." (No information)
    — Not: "The org needs to evolve." (Meaningless)

  For promotion denial: "The gap is [specific]. Here's a concrete example: [instance]."
    — Not: "The committee felt you weren't quite ready." (Who? What specifically?)
    — Not: "It was very close." (Not useful)

  For reorgs: "The reason for this change is [specific — business driver or leadership decision]."
    — If you don't know the full reason: say that. "I know the why as far as [X].
      I don't know the full picture — I'll tell you what I find out."

  What you cannot share: HR/legal constraints may limit specifics. Name the constraint
  rather than pretending the constraint doesn't exist.
  "There are legal considerations that limit what I can share about this process.
   What I can tell you is [what I can tell them]."

PART 3 — THE SPACE (silence is the message)
  After delivering the lead and the reason, stop talking.
  The recipient needs to process. They are not ready for next steps yet.
  Your job in this moment is not to fill the silence — it is to hold it.

  Common EM failure: talking to manage your own discomfort after delivering the message.
  This closes the space the person needs to react.
  
  What to say when they go quiet: Nothing. Or: "Take a moment."
  What to say if they cry: "Take your time." Or: "I'm sorry."
  What NOT to say: "I know this is hard, but..." or "I want you to know that..."
  (These redirect to your own perspective when they need to be in theirs.)

  Let them react. Reactions that are acceptable: silence, tears, anger, questions,
  disbelief, requests to end the conversation.
  Your job: stay present, stay calm, don't fill the space.

PART 4 — THE NEXT STEPS (clear, specific, owned)
  Once the person is ready (they will signal this by asking a question or reengaging):
  Name the next steps. Not options. Concrete steps.

  For layoffs: Last day. Access changes. Severance process. Reference commitment.
  For promotion denials: What the gap is. What closing it looks like. When the
    next opportunity is. What support the EM will provide.
  For reorgs: New reporting structure. Effective date. What happens to current work.
    Who to talk to with questions the EM can't answer.

  What they are looking for in next steps: "Do I have agency here? Is there
  anything I can do?" Give them the honest answer.
  If there is nothing they can do: "There isn't anything to do to change this decision.
  What I can do is [specific support]."
```

### Step 3 — Anticipate the Real Questions

```
Every message type generates predictable questions. Prepare answers before the room.

LAYOFF — the real questions
  "Why me?"
    Prepare: The specific criteria used to select this role for elimination.
    If you know: tell them.
    If you don't: "I wasn't in the room for every decision. What I know is [what you know]."
  "Can I appeal this?"
    Prepare: Whether there is an appeal process. The answer is usually no.
  "What will you say if someone asks you about me?"
    This is a reference question. Answer it directly and with commitment.
    "I will tell anyone who asks that [specific: their strongest qualities,
    what you'd recommend them for, how you'd describe their work]."
  "Did you fight for me?"
    This one is hard. Answer honestly.
    If you did: say what you did and what happened.
    If you didn't: own that. "I didn't have the standing / information / authority to..."
    Do not claim you fought for them if you didn't. They will find out.

PROMOTION DENIAL — the real questions
  "What exactly do I need to do?"
    Prepare: Three specific, observable behaviors at the next level. Not aspirational —
    behavioral. "You need to influence decisions cross-team without requiring escalation.
    Here's what that looks like in practice: [example]."
  "Is this fixable or is this a closed door?"
    Be honest. If they have a real path: say what it is and by when.
    If the ceiling is low in this org: say that too. "I want to be honest — the growth
    ceiling here is [X]. If that's not the trajectory you need, I'd rather you know that."
  "Was there advocacy for me?"
    Answer honestly. Not reassuringly.

REORG — the real questions
  "Was I consulted in this decision?"
    Answer honestly. "No / Yes, here's how."
  "What does this mean for my career path?"
    Prepare: Your honest read of the trajectory in the new structure.
  "Did you know this was coming?"
    Be honest. If you knew and couldn't say: own that. "I knew and I was asked to
    keep it confidential until it was announced. I'm sorry I couldn't tell you earlier."
    Do not pretend you didn't know if you did.
```

### Step 4 — Manage the Afterward

```
The conversation is not over when the meeting ends.

WITHIN THE SAME DAY
  — Send a brief written summary of what was discussed and the next steps.
    Not a legal document — a human record that shows you heard what was said.
  — If anyone on the team is aware of the message: ensure they hear it from the
    EM before they hear it secondhand.

WITHIN ONE WEEK
  — Follow up with the person. Not to check if they've "processed it" —
    to check if the next steps happened as promised.
  — If you promised a reference, an introduction, a plan: deliver it within one week.
    The credibility of what you said in the room depends on what you do after it.

TEAM COMMUNICATION (if message affects the team)
  — Inform the team promptly, factually, and with as much honesty as is authorized.
  — The team will know something happened. They will form narratives to explain
    what they don't know. Fill the vacuum with truth before anxiety fills it with fiction.
  — What to say: "{{Person}} is leaving [as of date]. I want to be direct: [the reason,
    at the level of specificity you can share]. Here's what changes for us: [practical impact].
    I'm available to answer questions."

THE REFERENCE CONVERSATION
  — Every person who leaves under a difficult message deserves a clear reference commitment.
  — Commit to what you will say, not just that you'll say something.
    "I will tell anyone who asks that you are [specific quality] and I'd recommend you for
    [specific type of role]. I will not disclose [what happened here] unless you ask me to."
  — Write this down and give it to them. It reduces anxiety and honors the relationship.
```

## Decision Tree

```
What does the difficult message delivery require?

├── MESSAGE HAS NOT BEEN FULLY PREPARED (reason not clear, next steps not known)
│   └── Stop. Do not enter the conversation without Part 1–4 of the architecture.
│       A difficult message delivered incompletely is worse than a delayed one.
│       Delay by one day to complete the preparation.
│       Exception: crisis-type situations where delay causes greater harm.

├── LEGAL/HR CONSTRAINTS LIMIT WHAT CAN BE SAID
│   └── Name the constraint, don't work around it.
│       "There are legal reasons I can't go into more detail about the process.
│       What I can tell you is [what you can share]."
│       Do not pretend constraints don't exist.

├── RECIPIENT WILL EXPECT THE OPPOSITE (they think they're being promoted / praised)
│   └── Highest-anxiety delivery scenario.
│       Expectation management required: Do not allow the expectation to sit
│       while delivering a different message. Name it directly.
│       "I know you may have been expecting a different conversation today."
│       Then deliver the lead. Do not let the expectation gap go unacknowledged.

├── RECIPIENT IS VISIBLY DISTRESSED DURING DELIVERY
│   └── Hold the space. Do not fill it.
│       Offer: "Take your time. We don't have to get through everything now."
│       If they cannot continue: schedule a follow-up within 24 hours for next steps.
│       Do not rush through next steps to complete the agenda when the person is not ready.

├── RECIPIENT CHALLENGES THE DECISION
│   └── Listen. Do not defend.
│       "I hear that this feels [unfair/unexpected/wrong]. I want to understand
│       your perspective."
│       After listening: be honest about whether the decision is final or re-openable.
│       If final: say so. "This decision is final. I wanted to be honest about that."
│       If there is a legitimate challenge path: tell them what it is and how to access it.

└── EM IS ASKED "DID YOU FIGHT FOR ME?"
    └── Answer honestly. This question is about integrity, not comfort.
        If you fought: name what you did and what happened.
        If you didn't: own it without deflecting.
        Do not claim advocacy you did not provide.
        The trust cost of a discovered lie is higher than the discomfort of an honest answer.
```

## Confidence Score

| Condition | Modifier |
|---|---|
| Lead sentence prepared and delivered directly | Base: 65% |
| Reason is specific and honest | +15% |
| Space held after delivery (silence not filled) | +10% |
| Real questions anticipated and answered honestly | +10% |
| Post-conversation actions completed within stated timeframe | +5% |
| Message delivered without the lead sentence (buried in preamble) | −25% |
| Real questions answered with vague reassurance | −20% |
| Space filled immediately after delivery | −10% |
| Post-conversation promises not delivered | −25% (trust-destroying) |

## Failure Modes

| Mode | Symptom | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Buried lead** | Two minutes of preamble before the message | First sentence is the message. Write it out before the conversation. |
| **Compassionate vagueness** | "The committee felt you weren't quite ready" | Specific evidence is more compassionate than comfortable vagueness. Name the gap. |
| **Space-filling** | EM talks through the recipient's reaction | After the message is delivered, stop. Hold the space. |
| **Advocacy overclaim** | "I really fought hard for you" when you didn't | Say what you actually did. The lie will be discovered and costs more than the truth. |
| **Promise default** | Vague "I'm here for you" without specific commitments | Name one specific thing you will do within one week. Then do it. |
